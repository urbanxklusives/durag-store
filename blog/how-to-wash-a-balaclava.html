How to Wash a Balaclava (and Keep It Warm, Soft and Shaped)

A balaclava does the hardest job in the winter kit: full coverage, trapping heat and moisture against your hair and skin for hours at a time. That is exactly why it needs washing more often than the rest of the rotation — sweat, skin oils, and cold-weather grime build up fast in a knit that never gets a break. Wash it right and it stays warm, soft, and shaped for seasons. Wash it wrong — hot water, a hot dryer, harsh detergent — and you get a shrunken, stiff, pill-covered thing that fits nobody. This is the routine that keeps it right.

Read the label first

Every balaclava has a care label, and it is the authority — knit and fleece do not want the same treatment, and blends vary. The label tells you the water temperature, whether the dryer is allowed, and whether bleach is safe (it almost never is). If the label is gone, treat the piece as delicate: cold water, gentle cycle, air dry. That default is safe for every common balaclava fabric, and it is the routine the rest of this guide assumes.

The wash

Turn the balaclava inside out before it goes in — the inside is where the sweat and oils live, and inside-out is where the detergent reaches them while the outside face stays protected. Wash cold, on a gentle cycle, with a mild detergent. A mesh laundry bag is worth it: it stops the balaclava tangling with zippers and hooks from the rest of the load, which is how face openings stretch and knits snag. Skip the bleach entirely, and skip fabric softener on fleece — softener coats the fibres and dulls the warmth you bought it for. Wash after heavy use, not after every light wear: a balaclava worn for a short cold walk can air out and go again, but a full day of sweat and wind earns a wash.

Drying: the step that ruins balaclavas

Heat is the enemy. A hot dryer shrinks knits, stiffens fleece, and kills elastic — the face opening is always the first casualty. Air dry instead: reshape the balaclava while it is still damp, pulling the face opening gently back to its shape, then lay it flat or hang it somewhere with airflow, out of direct heat and direct sun. If the label explicitly allows tumble drying, low heat only, and take it out while it is still slightly damp. Never iron a balaclava, and never dry it on a radiator. Patience here is the whole game: a balaclava dried slowly keeps its shape, and one dried hot never gets it back.

Odour, pilling, and stretched elastic

Odour that survives a wash usually lives in the fibres, not on them: air the balaclava out fully after every wear before it goes back in a drawer, and for stubborn smells, soak it in cold water with a spoonful of baking soda for half an hour before washing as normal. Pilling is normal wear on knit and fleece — use a fabric shaver or a lint roller, and never pull pills off by hand, which only thins the fabric further. A stretched face opening can often be rescued by reshaping while damp and drying flat; prevention is better — gentle wash, mesh bag, no dryer. If the elastic is genuinely gone, that is wear, not a wash mistake, and it is time for a replacement.

Storage and the layer underneath

Store a balaclava clean and fully dry — never damp in a drawer, where mildew finds it fast. Fold it loosely rather than stuffing it, so the face opening keeps its shape between wears. And one habit makes every wash easier: wear a thin cap underneath. The layer closest to your hair takes the oils and sweat, the balaclava itself stays cleaner for longer, and the layering logic is the same one that protects a wave pattern under a helmet — one layer for the hair, one layer for the weather.
